What it does

A plan says "all 33 KRs by the end of the quarter". Reality is a range. This calculator turns your confidence in each key result into an honest forecast: how many KRs you should expect to land, how likely you are to hit your target, and which items or risks move the answer most.

KR or task? A key result (KR) is an outcome the OKR commits to, such as "40% of users active weekly". Tasks are the pieces of work that deliver it, like Jira sub-tasks. Count whatever you report on: KRs for an OKR review, tasks or deliverables for a sprint or project. Pick it in What are you counting? and the labels change to match.

It works for any OKR, project or delivery plan with a list of items and a deadline. The page opens with an example plan loaded (a made-up Customer Platform Launch) so you can see it working. Replace it with your own.

The tools

Quick calculator"Roughly, are we OK?"Sort your KRs into Safe, Likely and At risk. You get an answer in a minute, no setup needed.
Completion odds"How many KRs will we land, and how likely is our target?"Monte Carlo simulation of the whole OKR, including risks that hit several KRs at once.
Critical path (PERT)"Will the longest chain of dependent work finish in time?"Three-point estimates per step give a chance of finishing by your date.
Weekly check"We finished 5 of 8 tasks this week. Should I escalate?"A simple hypothesis test that separates a normal bad week from a real slip.
Target proof test"Does our sample prove we hit 40%?"For rate-based KRs, such as "40% of records meet the standard": how many conforming items you need in a sample to claim the target.

Quick start (10 minutes)

Need a rough answer first? Open Quick calculator, type how many KRs are Safe, Likely and At risk, and read the answer. Open in full calculator turns it into a starting setup for the steps below.

  1. Name it. On Completion odds, type your OKR name and deadline under Your plan, and choose what you are counting.
  2. Set how many there are. Type the number in How many in total and press Update total to add or remove rows. Then set How many must land; it cannot be more than the total.
  3. Name your KRs. Edit the table directly, use Add KR, or open Paste KRs from Excel and paste three columns: ID, name, chance (%). Start blank clears the example.
  4. Set a chance for each KR. Your honest probability that it is approved by the deadline if nothing unusual goes wrong. Use the scale in Choosing good inputs.
  5. Add shared risks. These are things that would hit several KRs at once, such as "API access granted on time" or "business owners respond". Give each a probability. Then, in the KR table, put the chance a KR keeps if that risk goes wrong (for example 60 means a 90% KR drops to 54%). Leave it blank if the risk does not affect that KR.
  6. Read the result and compare scenarios. The numbers update as you type. Press Compare scenarios to see which risk is worth fixing first, then Copy summary to paste the result into an email or Teams.

Choosing good inputs

Use the same scale across the team so numbers mean the same thing.

ChanceUse it when
95–98%Routine or already mostly done; only an accident stops it.
90%Clear plan, clear owner, no outside dependency.
80–85%Depends on one other team, a licence or a sign-off you do not control.
70–75%Several dependencies, or the target is a stretch from today's baseline.
≤ 60%Needs things to go right that have gone wrong before. Consider rewording the KR.
  • Shared risk probability: the chance the risk goes your way (for example "partner API access by week 4": 75%).
  • Chance kept if the risk fails: a percentage of the KR's own chance. 100 or blank means no effect; 50 halves it; 0 means the KR cannot happen.
  • The risk is a KR itself: if a KR succeeds exactly when the risk goes well (in the example, B3 Partner API integration), pick it in Is also KR. That KR then follows the risk.
  • Chance if scope change refused: optional. If you have asked to reword some KRs, put the lower chance they would have unchanged, then untick Scope or rewording accepted to see the difference.

Reading the results

Expected KRs
The average number landed across all simulated quarters. Report this as your forecast, not the plan's total.
Chance of at least N
Share of simulated quarters that hit the target you set. Green is 90% or above, amber 80–89%, red below 80%.
P10 – P50 – P90
A bad quarter, a typical quarter and a good quarter. 80% of outcomes fall between P10 and P90.
Independent check
The same KRs with no shared risks. The gap between this and the simulation is how much correlated risk costs you.
Result per KR
Each KR's chance once shared risks are included. Sort by it to find the KRs to protect.
Scenario comparison
Re-runs the model with one risk removed at a time. The biggest jump is the risk to fix first.
PERT z and chance
How many standard deviations of slack you have, and the chance the chain finishes by the date.
p-value and α
The p-value is how surprising the result would be if things were on plan (weekly check) or if the target were not met (proof test). If it is at or below α (usually 0.05), act on it.

Worked example (loaded by default)

The made-up Customer Platform Launch has 33 KRs across seven objectives, 3 shared risks and an end-of-quarter deadline. B3 (Partner API integration) is both a KR and a shared risk at 75%, because the data pipeline (C3), real-time metrics (B4), integration monitoring (D4), partner feeds (D5) and the single customer view (B5) all depend on it.

Result with 200,000 runs: 29.3 of 33 expected, 89% chance of at least 27 (80%), 51% chance of at least 30, about 4% chance of all 33. Fixing B3 alone lifts the expected total to 30.0; fixing all three risks lifts it to 30.5.

Limits

  • The output is only as good as the chances you put in. Revisit them every week as evidence comes in.
  • Shared risks are either "went well" or "went badly" for the whole quarter. Partial slips are approximated by the chance kept.
  • PERT assumes steps run one after another and the total is roughly normal. Treat results below 50% as "will probably slip".
  • This is a planning aid, not a commitment. Use it to decide what to escalate, reword or protect.

    Key results

    All figures are percentages. "Kept if … fails" is the share of the KR's chance that survives when that risk goes badly. Blank means no effect.

    How the model works 
    For each simulated quarter:
  each shared risk r goes well with probability P(r)
  for each KR k:
    p = chance_k  (or chance-if-refused_k when scope is refused)
    if k is tied to risk r:   p = 1 if r went well, else 0
    for each risk r that went badly:   p = p × kept_k,r
    KR k lands with probability p
  X = number of KRs landed
Expected = average X;  P(X ≥ target) = share of quarters with X ≥ target
Independent check = exact Poisson-binomial on chance_k with no shared risks
